1. The Problem

Consider an organization that manages a fleet of drivers picking up and delivering items across a metro area. Orders arrive throughout the day with varying urgency levels, handling requirements, and deadlines. Each order originates at a pickup location and contains one or more packages, each potentially going to a different destination. A human dispatcher assigns drivers to orders one at a time, using their judgment to balance proximity, equipment fit, and urgency.

Why is this hard? This is a Vehicle Routing Problem with Pickup and Delivery (VRPPD) — NP-hard in the general case. The challenge has three layers:

  1. Assignment (who gets what): Match N drivers to M orders while respecting hard constraints (equipment, certifications, capacity, shift windows, deadlines) and minimizing total cost
  2. Pooling (can we batch?): Group compatible nearby orders for a single driver when detour cost is low, intentionally holding low-urgency orders to create batching opportunities
  3. Routing (in what order): Optimize each driver's multi-stop delivery sequence (per-driver TSP with precedence constraints: pickup before delivery)

A human dispatcher handles layer 1 reasonably well but can't simultaneously optimize across all three. Each individual decision looks good — send the nearest eligible driver — but the sequence of decisions is globally suboptimal. Assigning Driver A to Order X might be locally rational, but it leaves no feasible option for the higher-priority Order Y arriving 10 minutes later.

This simulator demonstrates the gap: how much is lost by sequential human decision-making vs. batch-optimal algorithmic dispatch.

5. The Simulator

The simulation uses real NYC coordinates for hubs, branches, destinations, and satellites plotted on OpenStreetMap. Here's what's generated:

  • Facilities: 8 hubs, 12 branches across Manhattan/Brooklyn/Queens, 6 destinations (Destination A, Destination B, Destination C), 3 satellites
  • Orders: Arrive in waves — morning rush (7-9am, 40%), midday (25%), afternoon spike (25%), late (10%)
  • Cargo mix: Standard 45%, Bulk 20%, Fragile 10%, Sensitive 10%, Cold Chain 10%, Ultra-Cold 5%
  • Urgency: STAT 15%, Urgent 25%, Routine 45%, Standard 15%
  • Multi-destination: 40% of orders have 1 package, 30% have 2, 20% have 3, 10% have 4
  • Drivers: 60% cars, 25% vans, 15% bikes. 40% ambient-only, 30% cooler, 20% active fridge, 10% cryo

Distance Model

Travel distances use the Haversine formula (great-circle distance) multiplied by a 1.4 road factor to approximate actual road distance in NYC's grid. Travel time assumes 25 km/h average urban speed (18 for bikes, 22 for vans). Deadline feasibility uses the 80th percentile of a log-normal travel time distribution — conservative estimates that account for traffic, parking, and handoff variability.

6. Algorithm 1: Smart Greedy (Competent Dispatcher)

This isn't a straw man — it represents what a good human dispatcher does, making reasonable decisions one order at a time:

What it does well:

  1. Sort orders by urgency (STAT first), then by deadline tightness within each tier
  2. Check all hard constraints (equipment, certifications, capacity, shift window)
  3. Score each eligible driver considering:
    • Distance (primary factor — the dispatcher looks at the map)
    • Deadline feasibility — heavily penalizes drivers who'd likely miss the deadline, gets cautious about tight ones (<15 min slack)
    • Equipment match — prefers not to waste a high-capability driver on a standard-tier order (but isn't perfect at this, especially under pressure)
  4. Pick the best-scoring driver. Move to the next order.

Where it still falls short (inherent limitations of sequential, one-at-a-time human decision-making):

  • No global view: Each assignment is locally rational but globally suboptimal. Assigning Driver A here — even with good equipment awareness — might leave no feasible option for Order Y arriving 10 minutes later. A human can't hold all N×M possibilities in their head.
  • Equipment preference, not optimization: The dispatcher tries not to waste the cryo driver, but under time pressure or when there are many orders, they'll still make suboptimal equipment matches. The Hungarian algorithm guarantees globally optimal equipment allocation.
  • No delivery route optimization: When an order has 3 packages going to 3 different destinations, the dispatcher just sends the driver in the order the destinations come up. The optimal algorithm uses nearest-neighbor + 2-opt to find the shortest delivery sequence.
  • No pooling: Every order gets its own dedicated driver. Two facilities 500m apart each get separate drivers, even though one trip could serve both.
  • Deadline slack isn't weighed against future demand: The dispatcher doesn't think "this Routine order has 3 hours of slack, so I should save this nearby driver for the STAT that's likely coming in 20 minutes."

7. Algorithm 2: Hungarian Optimal Assignment

Uses the Hungarian algorithm (scipy.optimize.linear_sum_assignment) to find the globally optimal driver-to-order matching:

  1. Build a cost matrix (drivers × orders) where each cell represents the "cost" of assigning that driver to that order
  2. Cost function considers multiple factors:
    • Distance to pickup × urgency multiplier (STAT = 3x weight)
    • Infinity (∞) if hard constraints are violated (wrong equipment, no cert, over capacity)
    • Deadline risk penalty (proportional to minutes of negative slack)
    • Over-qualification penalty (don't waste cryo driver on ambient orders)
    • Multi-destination delivery route distance
  3. Solve: Hungarian algorithm finds the assignment that minimizes total cost across ALL driver-order pairs simultaneously (O(n³) time)
  4. Optimize routes: For each assigned driver, use nearest-neighbor + 2-opt to find the best delivery stop sequence

The 2-opt improvement iteratively swaps pairs of edges in the route to eliminate crossings, respecting the constraint that pickups must come before their corresponding deliveries.

8. Pooling & Insertion (Advanced)

Order Pooling: Groups nearby compatible orders into batches. A driver picking up from Branch A can also grab items from Branch B (0.5 km away) on the same trip. Pooling criteria:

  • Pickup locations within 2 km
  • Temperature-compatible items (can't pool "must not freeze" sensitive items with frozen items)
  • Combined packages fit within driver capacity

Slack-based holding: Routine orders can wait 15-30 minutes for a pooling match. STAT orders dispatch immediately. This mimics how UberEats holds your order for a minute waiting for a second delivery nearby.

Cheapest Insertion: When a new order arrives while drivers are mid-route, the system evaluates inserting it into an existing route vs. dispatching a new driver. If the detour is small and deadlines still met, it inserts — saving a whole dispatch.

9. Assumptions & Simplifications

This is a simulation prototype, not a production system. The following assumptions and simplifications are made intentionally — each represents a known upgrade path:

Distance & Travel Time

  • No real routing API: Distances use the Haversine formula (great-circle) × 1.4 road factor. For NYC's grid street layout, this approximates actual road distance within ~15%. A production system would use OSRM, Google Maps, or Mapbox for exact routing.
  • Constant base speed: 25 km/h for cars, 22 for vans, 18 for bikes — reasonable NYC urban averages. No time-of-day traffic model. However, stochastic variability is modeled: travel times are sampled from a log-normal distribution (CV=0.20 normal, 0.35 rush hour) to capture real-world uncertainty.
  • Conservative feasibility: Deadline checks use the 80th percentile of the travel time distribution, not the mean. This means the algorithm won't create routes that only work if everything goes perfectly.
  • Stop buffer times: 5 minutes per pickup (handoff, paperwork, scanning) and 3 minutes per delivery. These are configurable constants, not measured from data.

Scenario Structure

  • Static snapshot: All orders and drivers are known at scenario generation time. In production, orders arrive dynamically — the insertion algorithm handles this, but the demo runs in batch mode for clarity.
  • No central depot: Drivers start at scattered positions across the city (some already en-route). This is more realistic than a single-depot model but means there's no "return to base" cost.
  • One order per driver (greedy): The greedy baseline assigns exactly one order per driver. The optimal algorithm does the same for fair comparison, but the pooling module demonstrates multi-order batching separately.
  • Generated data, not historical: Order arrival patterns (wave-based), cargo type mix, urgency distribution, and driver capabilities are generated from configured probability distributions — not learned from real operational data. The distributions are based on industry-typical patterns.

Constraints

  • Hard constraints are strict: Temperature equipment, certifications, capacity, shift windows, and deadline feasibility are binary pass/fail. No "soft" constraint relaxation (e.g., "deliver 10 minutes late if it saves 30 km").
  • Temperature compatibility is simplified: Items that "must not freeze" cannot ride with frozen/cryo items. In practice, multi-compartment vehicles or insulated packaging could allow co-transport.
  • No driver breaks or refueling: Drivers are available continuously within their shift window.

What This Prototype Does NOT Include

  • Real-time GPS tracking or live driver position updates
  • Historical demand prediction or machine learning
  • Driver mobile app or turn-by-turn navigation
  • Multi-period planning (this solves one dispatch window at a time)
  • Vehicle return-to-depot or end-of-shift routing
  • Google OR-Tools or commercial VRP solver (uses scipy Hungarian + custom heuristics)

10. The Roadmap

  • Google OR-Tools VRP solver: Joint optimization of assignment + routing + pooling
  • Real-time GPS integration: Use actual driver positions, not simulated
  • Predictive demand modeling: Learn demand patterns by time of day — pre-position drivers
  • Tracking and chain of custody: Scan at pickup, scan at delivery — full audit trail
  • Driver mobile app: Turn-by-turn navigation with optimized stop sequence
